About South Frankfort

The National Park Service nomination for the South Frankfort Historic District describes about 183 acres and about 660 buildings, predominantly residential. Most of the district dates from the late 19th and early 20th centuries, and the nomination says the area was settled before 1800 around a river ferry. It became a separate corporation in 1812, and growth picked up after 1850.

According to the same nomination, T-plan houses are the most common type, with Queen Anne houses prevalent in the center of the district. Lots are small in the eastern and southwestern parts. The district was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 1982, with a boundary increase in 2013.

Older streets, water and sewer service

The City of Frankfort's flood protection guide names South Frankfort among the older sections of town where combined sewer overflows can cause flooding. Combined systems carry stormwater and household wastewater in the same pipes, so a heavy rain can push water back toward low drains and basements.

A State Journal report on the April 2025 river crest recorded a stage of 48.27 feet, a near-record level, and noted that basement flooding typically begins around 39 feet. That context makes backwater and sump questions worth asking before the next high-water season.

A water main break the neighborhood has seen

On June 27, 2022, a cast iron water main on Fourth Street, between Steele and Conway, failed, and about five blocks lost water pressure. The Frankfort Plant Board repaired the line and replaced that section with ductile iron.

A public main is the utility's job, and a break on your side of the meter is the owner's. If you lose pressure, check whether neighbors are affected first. If they are, call the utility. If only your home is affected, call us and we will trace the problem.

Practical guidance if river or storm water reaches your drains

The city's guide says a plug or standpipe can be used for one to two feet of sewer backup, and that deeper backup calls for an overhead sewer or a backup valve. These are general options. A plumber has to look at your fixtures and the pipe layout before recommending one.

Standard homeowner policies usually exclude flood damage, and the National Flood Insurance Program covers it separately. Ask your insurance agent what your policy covers before the next high-water season.
