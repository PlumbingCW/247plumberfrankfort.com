Using a Tankless Water Heater for Radiant Heat in Frankfort, KY Homes

Some tankless heaters can heat a radiant floor loop. Many cannot. The difference matters for warranty, safety and comfort.

Call (855) 575-2890

By the Frankfort 24/7 Plumbing team. Published 2026-11-16.

Radiant floor heat circulates warm water through tubes in or under the floor. Some homeowners ask whether a tankless water heater can supply both the taps and the floor loop. The answer is yes for certain combination units that are designed for it, and no for most standard tankless units. Using the wrong heater can void the warranty and can put untreated water into a closed loop.

This guide explains the main system layouts, what to ask a manufacturer and why a planned design matters more than the equipment price. It does not replace a design by a licensed professional, and it does not cover every possible layout.

Three ways to run radiant heat with hot water

LayoutHow it worksPoints to check
Open loopDomestic hot water runs directly through the floor loopMany codes and manufacturers restrict this because the water can stagnate and carry contaminants.
Closed loop with a heat exchangerA heat exchanger separates domestic water from the floor loopNeeds a heat exchanger and a circulation pump.
Combination boilerA boiler designed for both space heat and domestic hot waterOften the cleanest design for new construction.
Separate boiler for the floor loopA dedicated boiler runs the loop and a tankless unit handles tapsClear separation, more equipment.

Why a standard tankless unit is not enough

A tankless heater is built to raise the temperature of water that flows through it once. A radiant loop recirculates water over and over, and it demands steady heat for hours. The two duties are different, and a unit rated for domestic hot water only may not carry a long space heating load.

A unit that is used outside its design can short-cycle, wear out its heat exchanger early or fail to meet the load on a cold day. Some manufacturers publish combination models rated for space heating and domestic hot water together. Ask for the data sheet that states radiant heating as an approved use.

Questions to answer before you buy equipment

  • What is the heat load of the area, based on a calculation and not a guess?
  • Does the manufacturer list radiant floor heat as an approved use for this model?
  • Will the design use a heat exchanger or a closed loop with antifreeze or treated water?
  • Is there a gas line, a vent route and the electrical supply the unit needs?
  • How will you drain and flush the unit, and where will condensate go?
  • What happens in a power outage? A circulating pump and a control board both need power.

Scale, water quality and long-term care

Closed loops reduce scale because the same water circulates. The domestic side of a tankless unit still sees fresh water and scales over time, so a flush on a schedule is a must. Frankfort Plant Board's published water quality report does not give a hardness figure, so ask the utility or test your tap, then set the flush interval.

Floor type, tube spacing and water temperature

Radiant floors differ in how fast they respond. Tubes embedded in concrete store heat and respond slowly, while tubes under a wood subfloor respond faster but need hotter water. The floor covering matters too, since tile moves heat well and thick carpet slows it down.

Water temperature in the loop affects efficiency. Lower loop temperatures work with condensing equipment, and mixing valves hold the loop at the right setting while the heater runs hotter. A designer sets these numbers from the floor type, so they are not left to guesswork.

Winter conditions around Frankfort

At Capital City Airport the average January low is about 25 degrees Fahrenheit, and the record low is far below zero. Downtown readings run a few degrees colder. A radiant system is a way to keep a floor warm in that climate, and a design should account for the coldest nights and for power outages that stop a pump.
